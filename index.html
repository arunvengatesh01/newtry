Sweetener

We use jaggery instead of white sugar

1. What is jaggery?

Jaggery (gur) is made by boiling sugarcane juice until it thickens, then moulding it, with no spinning and no removal of molasses. That is why it is brown and keeps trace minerals that white sugar loses.

2. Why it matters for health

  • Contains small amounts of iron, potassium and magnesium that refined sugar does not.
  • Molasses brings polyphenol antioxidants.
  • Gives a rounder, caramel flavour, so less total sweetener is needed.

6. One biscuit with jaggery

Assumption: a 15 g biscuit with about 20% sweetener = 3 g jaggery.

  • ≈ 11 kcal of slow-flavoured energy, plus roughly 0.1–0.3 mg iron and small potassium and magnesium traces.
  • Fewer ultra-processed sweetness spikes, since the molasses brings flavour depth.
  • 3 biscuits a day ≈ 9 g, or about 3.3 kg jaggery per year.

Important note: jaggery is still sugar. The benefit is a better-quality swap, not a licence to eat more.

7. Same biscuit with white sugar

  • 3 g white sugar ≈ 12 kcal with zero minerals; 3 biscuits a day ≈ 3.3 kg per year of pure sucrose.
  • Possible future problems: dental caries, weight gain, fatty liver and reduced insulin sensitivity when total sugar intake stays high.

9. Rare information

  • A darker jaggery is not automatically richer in minerals; the cane variety and soil matter more.
  • Jaggery was once stored as a “keeping sweet” for months because its low water activity resists microbes.
  • Indian white sugar is often “plantation white”: bleached with sulphur dioxide rather than bone char.
Refining

What is refining?

1–2. What and why

Refining is an industrial process that strips a natural food down to one pure component: bran and germ removed from wheat, molasses removed from sugar, free fatty acids and colour removed from oil. Companies do it to get uniform colour, a neutral taste, a longer shelf life and lower costs in bulk.

For sugar: juice is limed, bleached, crystallised, spun in a centrifuge and dried.

3. What jaggery loses when refined

  • Molasses, the source of iron, potassium, calcium and magnesium
  • Polyphenol antioxidants
  • Trace B vitamins and the natural caramel flavour

4. The state after refining

It becomes white crystalline sucrose, commonly called refined sugar: nearly pure carbohydrate with no other nutrients. Refined flour (maida) is the equivalent for wheat.

5. Why companies choose this state

  • Cheaper per kilo and identical in every batch
  • Stable for years, so long supply chains work
  • Dissolves cleanly and gives pale biscuits that look “premium”

6. Rare points

  • The molasses removed is sold on separately for animal feed, rum and fermentation.
  • Refined carbohydrate digests fast, so blood glucose rises sooner than with whole foods.
  • Appearance, not nutrition, is the main reason white became the “standard”.
Oil

We strictly avoid palm oil

Why palm oil is not good for health

  • About 50% saturated fat, mostly palmitic acid (~44%), which raises LDL (“bad”) cholesterol.
  • Refined palm oil is deodorised at over 200 °C, which forms contaminants called 3-MCPD esters and glycidyl esters; food safety authorities flag these.
  • Cheap to buy and solid at room temperature, which is why industry loves it, not because it is good for you.
  • Repeated heating raises oxidised fats, which stress blood vessels.

What we use instead

Cold-pressed and expeller-pressed unsaturated oils such as rice bran oil (with natural oryzanol), high-oleic sunflower oil and groundnut oil: rich in mono- and polyunsaturated fatty acids, lower in saturated fat, with natural tocopherol antioxidants.

If you eat palm-oil biscuits, 2–3 a day, for 365 days

Colours

The dark side of artificial colours

  • Many are synthetic azo dyes made from petroleum-derived chemicals; they add zero nutrition and exist only to make food look brighter.
  • A 2007 University of Southampton study linked mixes of some dyes with a preservative to increased hyperactivity in children.
  • The EU requires a warning label (“may have an adverse effect on activity and attention in children”) on foods containing six of them.
  • Some can trigger hives or asthma in sensitive people.
  • Dyes let makers hide low-quality ingredients behind a rich colour.

Preservatives

What are preservatives?

1. Definition

Substances added to slow spoilage by microbes or oxidation: e.g. calcium propionate (E282), potassium sorbate (E202), sodium benzoate (E211), BHA (E320), BHT (E321) and TBHQ (E319).

2. Side effects

  • Sodium benzoate combined with some dyes was linked to hyperactivity in children.
  • BHA is classed by the IARC as “possibly carcinogenic” (Group 2B).
  • Propionates have caused irritability and sleep issues in some children, and a 2019 study linked propionate to glucose and insulin disruption.
  • Some people react with asthma or skin allergies.

3. Why branded companies use them

  • Biscuits must survive months of transport and shelf time.
  • Fewer returns, less wastage, higher margin.
  • They allow cheaper fats that would otherwise turn rancid.

4. Rare points

  • Rancidity is oxidation of fats, which is why synthetic antioxidants such as BHA and TBHQ go into fat-heavy products.
  • Our approach: low water activity (jaggery), stable unsaturated oils with natural tocopherols, careful baking, protective packaging, and shorter shelf life by design.
